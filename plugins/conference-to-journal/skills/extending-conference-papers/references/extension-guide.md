## What counts as new material

Editors judge new contributions. A useful test: can the authors name a finding, method, or result in the journal version that a reader of the conference paper would not already know? If the honest answer is only "more detail", the extension may not meet the venue's bar, and the user should hear that early.

Usually counts as new material:
- New experiments, datasets, or evaluations, including real deployments and user studies
- New or extended methods, algorithms, or system components
- New theory: proofs, bounds, or formal results (including proofs omitted for space, if the venue counts them)
- New analysis of existing data: ablations, sensitivity studies, failure analysis
- New application domains or research questions

Usually welcome but counts for less (some venues, such as STVR, exclude it outright):
- Longer background, motivation, and related work
- Fuller method descriptions and more examples
- Expanded discussion and limitations

Added length without new findings does not count.

## Finding the extension

Look for candidates in:
1. The conference paper's limitations and future work sections
2. The conference reviews, especially requests the page limit forced the authors to defer
3. Material cut for the page limit
4. Data or experiments the authors have collected since

For each candidate, record what it adds, whether it is a contribution or an elaboration, what the authors still need to do, and where it goes in the paper. Then write a two-sentence statement of the increment: what is new, and why it matters.

## Required changes to the paper

- Title: new, or at least different, reflecting the broader contribution.
- Abstract: rewrite it around the journal version's contributions. Many venues do not accept the old abstract with a sentence appended.
- Introduction: cite the conference paper, state the relationship, and list the new contributions explicitly.
- Related work: add work published since the conference paper, and position the journal version against it.
- Results, discussion, and conclusion: reflect the new material, and update claims the new results change.
- Disclosure: follow the venue's format (introduction sentence, title-page footnote, or acknowledgment), and fill in the submission system's prior-publication field.
- Files: many venues want a copy of the conference paper, a summary of differences, and a cover letter.

## Reused text and similarity checks

Many venues allow reused text and figures from the authors' own conference paper (IEEE ComSoc says so explicitly), and similarity checks will flag them. Disclosure is what makes reuse acceptable. Do not reword old text only to lower a similarity score: it adds no material, and it can look like concealment. Spend the effort on new content and a clear summary of differences instead.

## Reuse rights

The conference paper's publisher often holds its copyright, and that publisher may differ from the journal's. Check what the conference publisher allows:
- ACM: authors may reuse any part of their own work in a new work of their own, with a citation and a DOI pointer to the ACM version of record.
- Springer proceedings: the consent form permits an extended journal version that meets the three conditions in venue-policies.md.
- Others: read the copyright or license agreement, or the publisher's author-rights page. Figures from other authors' work need their own permission.

## Special cases

- Invited special issues: the call's rules (threshold, deadlines, review process) usually override the journal's general policy.
- Several conference papers combined into one journal article: some venues accept consolidation with significant new material; others refuse it. Check before planning.
- Journal version submitted before or alongside the conference version: some societies allow this with conditions. Check the policy.
- Workshop papers without formal proceedings: often not treated as prior publication, but still disclose them.
